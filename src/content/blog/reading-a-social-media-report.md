A social media report is not a scoreboard. It is a short explanation of what happened, what it might mean and what should change.

The best reports are often one page. They respect the reader's time, show the important context and make the next decision obvious.

## Start with the question someone asked

Every report should answer a question. Examples:

- Did the campaign reach the audience we intended?
- Which content formats produced useful engagement?
- Did the increase in posting frequency improve anything?
- What should we change next month?

If nobody asked a question, write one before you start. A report without a question tends to become a tour of every number the platform provides.

## Separate reach, engagement and outcome

Group the numbers into three layers so the reader can follow the story:

1. **Reach:** impressions, reach, views, profile visits.
2. **Engagement:** saves, shares, replies, comments, clicks, watch time.
3. **Outcome:** the action that matters to the business, such as a sign-up, enquiry or qualified visit.

Not every post will have an outcome number. That is normal. The point is to avoid treating reach as if it were an outcome.

## Put the numbers in context

A number on its own is hard to interpret. Add the comparison that makes it useful:

- compared with the previous period;
- compared with the target;
- compared with a different format or audience;
- compared with the same period last year, if the data is available and fair.

Also note changes that could affect the comparison: a holiday, a launch, a tracking change or a different posting schedule. Context is not an excuse. It is part of the result.

## Look for the story in the exceptions

Averages can hide useful detail. Look for:

- the post that performed very differently from the rest;
- the audience segment that responded more than expected;
- the format that created saves but few clicks;
- the week where reach fell after a change;
- the comment that revealed a misunderstanding.

You do not need to explain every exception. Pick the one or two that could change a decision.

## Write a one-page summary

A useful structure is:

> **The question.** What were we trying to learn?
>
> **What happened.** Three to five key numbers with comparisons.
>
> **What it means.** Your interpretation, with uncertainty where it exists.
>
> **What we will do next.** One or two specific actions.

Keep the language plain. If a sentence needs a second read, simplify it. If a chart does not support a decision, move it to an appendix or leave it out.

## Make the next action obvious

A report is more likely to be used if it ends with a clear next step:

- keep the format and test a new topic;
- pause the channel that produced no useful action;
- fix the tracking before the next campaign;
- ask the sales team whether the leads were relevant;
- run a small test next month.

A next step should be specific enough that someone can own it.

## A short template you can adapt

- **Period:** dates covered.
- **Question:** the decision the report supports.
- **Headline:** one sentence summary.
- **Three numbers:** with context.
- **What changed:** notable events or tracking changes.
- **What we learned:** one or two observations.
- **Next actions:** owner and timing.
- **Appendix:** supporting charts and definitions.

The format is less important than the habit: start with a question, show context and end with a decision. That is what turns reporting into analysis.
