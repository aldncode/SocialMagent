Interview preparation is not about having the perfect answer. It is about reducing avoidable uncertainty so you can think clearly in the room.

This checklist is designed for early-career and mid-level digital marketing, social media and analytics roles. Adapt it to the job description rather than treating it as a script.

## Before you apply

- Read the job description twice. Highlight the three responsibilities that appear most often.
- Check whether your CV shows evidence for those responsibilities.
- Save a copy of the exact job description. It may disappear after the application closes.
- Note the tools mentioned. Be ready to explain your real level of comfort with each one.
- Check the company's own website and recent public content. Look for what they sell, who they serve and how they talk about it.

## Research that actually helps

Research is useful when it changes something you say or ask. Try to find:

- **The audience:** who does the business serve?
- **The offer:** what is being sold, and how does someone buy it?
- **The channel mix:** where does the business seem to invest attention?
- **The language:** how does the team describe its work?
- **The gaps:** what would you want to understand better?

Do not arrive with a list of criticisms. Arrive with a few thoughtful observations and questions.

## Prepare stories, not scripts

Write brief notes for six to eight real situations:

- a project you planned and delivered;
- a mistake you made and what changed afterwards;
- a time you had to prioritise with limited time;
- a piece of feedback that improved your work;
- a time you had to explain data to someone non-technical;
- a time you worked with someone who saw the problem differently.

For each one, note the situation, your decision, the action and the outcome. Keep the outcome honest. "I learned to check the tracking before launch" is a valid outcome.

## Practice the practical tasks

Many marketing interviews include a small task. You might be asked to critique a landing page, plan a week of content, interpret a dashboard or write a short campaign brief.

Practice these slowly:

- **Content plan:** choose a real brand you admire and outline one week of posts with a reason for each.
- **Analytics:** take a simple public dataset and write three observations and one recommended action.
- **Writing:** explain one marketing concept in plain language in under 150 words.
- **Presentation:** talk through a piece of your own work for three minutes.

The aim is not to guess the exact task. It is to get comfortable structuring your thinking out loud.

## Questions to ask the interviewer

Prepare more questions than you need. Good questions show that you are evaluating the role too.

- What does success look like in the first three months?
- Which metrics does the team use to judge this work?
- Who approves content and how long does that usually take?
- What tools does the team use day to day?
- What is one thing the team is trying to improve this year?
- How is feedback given in the team?

Avoid questions whose answers are clearly on the website, and avoid turning the interview into an interrogation. Two or three genuine questions are usually enough.

## After the interview

- Write down what you were asked while it is fresh.
- Note one thing you would answer differently next time.
- Send a short thank-you email if that is normal in your market.
- Follow up once if you were given a timeline and it has passed.

## A final note on honesty

If you do not know something, say so and explain how you would find out. Interviewers are often testing how you think, not whether you have memorised a definition.

Prepared does not mean polished. It means you have done enough thinking to be present.
